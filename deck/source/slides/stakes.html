Problem

03

Construction failures put billions of lender capital at risk.
If a project fails, the lender must know the cost to finish.

A failed project leaves the lender with unfinished work, an unclear picture of the project, extra cost to finish and an uncertain recovery. Each decision from there, to fund, intervene or sell, rests on knowing where the project stands.

$3.08bn

Secured lender exposure

When Bathla entered administration in August 2026, of $3.4bn owed in all. About 40 private credit funds exposed; realised losses are still undetermined (reported)

$736M

Still being reconciled

Inter-company balances the administrator thought might be overstated, a month after appointment. How the projects really stood was not known (reported)

20–40%

Replacement-builder premium

Additional cost on the remaining works of a distressed project, from founder experience. Part of it prices what the replacement cannot see.

The consequence: not knowing where a project stands can mean further funding, enforcement costs and credit losses. IRIS reads each funded project’s builder records: what trades are owed against what was claimed and certified, and cost against forecast. The group’s ledger is the administrator’s job.

Sources: The Urban Developer, 25 September 2026; ABC News, 17 October 2026; ASIC; founder observations. Detail on appendix A6.

Bathla figures from the administrator's updates as reported by The Urban Developer on 25 September 2026: about $3.08bn owed to secured lenders within about $3.4bn of total creditor debt, including about $145m to the ATO and $130m to unsecured creditors; a preliminary portfolio of about 219 projects carrying about $3.13bn of debt against an estimated $4.87bn of value, subject to review; and about $736m of inter-company receivables and payables that may be overstated and were still being reconciled. All 13 remaining active sites were closed on 23 September after emergency lender funding of $3m to $5m from five lenders ran out; the NSW Supreme Court extended the convening period to 13 September 2027. One lender's senior loan on a near-complete project was later sold and a receiver appointed to settle presales (ABC News, 17 October 2026). The $3.3bn figure used elsewhere in the deck is the AFR's total; this slide uses the secured-lender figure. Construction's share of insolvencies, 24.5 per cent in FY2025-26, is on slide 2 and A6 (ASIC). The replacement-builder premium of 20 to 40 per cent is founder experience, not a measured figure; part of it prices what the incoming builder cannot see in the records. Earlier context: a lender with a decade-long Bathla relationship refinanced out between late 2024 and January 2026; about 12 other lenders refinanced the projects, and one took over a 339-apartment project at 83 per cent complete in December 2025, rating non-completion risk low to medium (The Urban Developer, 25 August 2026; Rampart, 6 September 2026). ASIC's REP 820 found most private credit funds lacked effective separation between investment decisions and the monitoring of loan value. Scope of the example: IRIS reads a project's builder systems, so on a Bathla project it would show that project's trades owed against what was claimed and certified, and its cost position against forecast; it would not show the group's inter-company position across 540 entities, which is the administrator's reconciliation. The $736M figure is on the slide as the measure of how little was known a month in, not as something IRIS addresses.
