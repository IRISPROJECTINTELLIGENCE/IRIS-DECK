A9

Backs the references to distressed-project assessments on slides 2, 3, 6, 10 and 11. Proposed and not yet demonstrated: the platform is proven on builder pilots and shaped with a lender design partner first; assessments are offered as part of scaling, at a separate fee per engagement, because distress is where the information problem is most expensive and where a lender already pays outside advisers. The first assessment is a controlled one on a historic record set, scored against the outcome reached, before any live engagement. Fee: fixed per engagement and no share of the recovery, so IRIS has no stake in what it reports; $35,000 in the model, benchmarked to QS report fees; about 14 engagements in Year 5, about 2% of revenue. What it gives: where the project stands across the delivery domains, each finding valued, dated and linked to its record, and a register of gaps. An incoming builder will not take liability for the failed builder’s work, so it prices what it cannot see; a position traced to the records, with gaps listed, narrows what it has to price blind. Speed: a first position in days from access is the design aim; it depends on how complete the records are. Access: before an appointment, the facility’s information rights and the builder’s side deed; after one, a receiver appointed over the developer takes the developer’s rights under the head contract and can give consent, and if the builder is in administration its administrator controls its books and software subscriptions, so access needs the administrator’s agreement. The strongest case is IRIS on the project before a failure. What stays with others: the QS, the valuer and the insolvency practitioner; IRIS does not advise on the decision, value the asset, certify cost to complete or assess the builder’s solvency. The legal pathway is confirmed with lawyers at the month-9 opinion; not legal advice.
