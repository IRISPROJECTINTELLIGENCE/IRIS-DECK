On three real jobs, the funder was lending against a figure
the builder’s records had already moved past.

Real case studies with indicative data. 

These three jobs show the problem. IRIS has not been run on them yet; they are the first record sets the enterprise product is tested on.

Project A · $45M mixed-use

Open-book builder-developer; private credit facility arrived mid-build

$4.4M

The ledger showed $4.4M more cost than the developer’s and lender’s pack, at a third complete; a $1.5M delay claim not yet submitted; $5.8M of scope not yet let. Reached the funder: about nine months later. Decision it bore on: was the facility in balance.

Project B · $300M resi tower

Major builder; refinance at two-thirds complete

$0

The register showed the delay-incentive pool spent to the dollar against the forecast delay, with no weather buffer, on a $110M contract carrying $35M of change. Reached the funder: one monthly pack late, more than 30 times. Decision it bore on: the refinance terms.

Project C · $15M commercial

Investor syndicate with a private financier and QS-gated drawdowns

7 vs 15

The builder’s own report showed 7 days of delay allowance left. The lender’s QS report that month showed 15, the same as the month before. Reached the funder: not through the QS report. Decision it bore on: whether the completion date would hold.

Three private record sets, held by the founders and read in full: a builder's share drive (cost-system exports, registers, claims, programmes, cashflows, PCG reports, the lender's QS initial report and the bank's term sheet); a developer's PCG pack and its capital partner's feasibility; a lender's QS drawdown report, payment certificates, PCG to the financier and trust investor report. Figures, contract sizes, delivery-model labels and lags on the slide are altered so the projects cannot be identified; the structure of each issue is as found. Project A is a builder-developer job where the builder's internal forecast ran ahead of the figure in the developer's PCG pack for about a year, with a large unsubmitted delay claim, unlet finishes trades forecast flat at budget, and a programme sliding month on month. Project B is a major-builder tower under a well-run monthly PCG whose delay-incentive pool had been consumed to the dollar by approved wet-weather days, with the bank refinancing in at about three-quarters complete. Project C is a small commercial job across two contracts under a private financier with QS-gated drawdowns across two contracts, where, in one month, the builder's own report and the lender's QS report carried different counts of delay allowance remaining, the QS report repeating its wording from the month before; three months later the builder's report showed one day left with more than a third of the programme to run. The job still reached practical completion on time, which the lender could not have read from its report. On this job six recurring reports carried the same figures to different readers (the builder's monthly report, the project manager's client report and site report, the developer's report to the financier, the lender's QS drawdown report and the investor report), each drawing on another by hand. The monthly dashboard was typed by hand from the builder's PDF, with formula errors found in the working files. For a reader outside construction: in each case the number that decided whether the lender's money was safe existed in a record before it existed in a report. These are illustrative examples of the problem; they are not outcomes IRIS has delivered.
