Since 2021 the structures carrying construction risk have shifted.
These shifts have increased project opacity and risk.

A fixed price puts cost risk on a builder with a balance sheet; presales cover the exit. The lender watches the head contract.

Builder fails: the developer appoints another. Developer fails: the lender steps in and a solvent builder keeps building.

Observed since 2021

Fixed prices. The 2021–23 cost shock broke builders holding them; survivors price the risk higher or share it through cost-plus terms.

Builder capacity. Thinner balance sheets absorb less of an overrun before it reaches the project and the facility.

Delivery models. More in-house and JV building: one pool of equity takes the loss, and step-in may find no solvent builder.

Funding. Lenders requiring zero presales: 18% in 2023, 37% in 2026; those wanting 60–100%: 45% to 8% (Stamford Capital).

Each shift moves risk between the parties. The lender’s view stays the same: a certificate on the head contract.

These are market developments; many jobs still run the arm’s-length model. The implication holds across all of them: the lender needs to see how the project stands beneath the head contract, whatever the structure above it.

Step-in. Under the builder’s side deed, a lender can take the developer’s place if the developer defaults, and a solvent builder keeps building. In-house or JV, builder and developer are related entities or co-investors rather than one balance sheet: the builder’s margin and the developer’s equity are the same group capital, so a project loss has no second balance sheet to land on, and the builder tends to fail with the developer; Bathla’s in-house builder entered administration with the group on 25 August 2026. The share of jobs delivered in-house or by JV is not measured; it is observed across the founders’ own work and the failures listed. Step-in rights are excluded from the ipso facto stay, so the lender keeps them, but the builder can’t perform. The same stay stops the builder being terminated merely for its administration, so a fresh default is needed first. The builder’s records sit with its administrator, who can refuse a creditor’s requests, and group-level support fails with the group. In practice lenders appoint receivers, fund a replacement builder at a premium, and sometimes pay trades directly. At Bathla, lender emergency funding kept about 14 projects going in early September; when no further funding was agreed, the remaining 13 active sites closed and lenders began taking control of individual projects through receivers (The Urban Developer, 25 September 2026). Fixed-price failures 2022–25: Probuild, Condev, PBS, Porter Davis, St Hilliers, Roberts Co (reported). Funding: Stamford Capital’s Real Estate Debt Capital Markets Survey, run across March and April 2026 among major banks, second-tier banks, non-banks and private lenders, found 37% of construction lenders now require zero presales, up from 29% in 2025 and 18% in 2023, while the share requiring 60 to 100% presales fell from about 45% in 2021 to 8.1%; 94.5% plan to grow their loan books (The Urban Developer, 2026). Non-banks’ share of commercial property debt rose from about 10% to 16% between 2020 and 2024 (Foresight Analytics for Payton Capital), and the RBA noted modestly easier terms for developers in March 2026.
