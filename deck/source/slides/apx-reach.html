A4

Backs slide 10, which shows the chain of documents: drawdown condition, tender condition, head contract clause, side deed. Row 1: the lender’s QS report is required by the facility and paid for by the borrower, so a lender-required, borrower-paid report on the project is established practice. A payment-control vendor has also said that some lenders make its product a condition of lending on certain jobs (vendor claim, November 2025). Row 2: the facility agreement binds the borrower. The builder is bound by what it signs: the tender and award conditions, the head contract, and the builder’s side deed (also called a tripartite deed), signed by lender, developer and builder at financial close, which lenders already require. An IRIS clause sits there: read-only API access to that project’s systems, for monitoring, for that project. Row 3: a builder on a job under way owes nothing new, so the first conditions go on new facilities, extensions and refinances, when the loan is re-papered. Row 4: reasons to refuse are removed up front. Nothing is written back, the builder sees findings before the lender, the view covers the funded project and nothing beyond it, and the lender sees whether the job is forecast to lose money and by how much, while rates and subcontract prices stay with the builder. That a refusal reads as a signal to the lender is a hypothesis until a lender adopts. Row 5: the builder’s duty stands on its own and is not a precondition to its payment claims, because a contractual precondition to payment claims was held void in BRB Modular v AWX [2015] QSC 218. The drawdown condition is met by an exported pack, so a builder cannot hold up the developer’s drawdown. Subcontract confidentiality terms decide what trade data can be shown. Each lender adopts the condition on its own terms. To be confirmed with lawyers at the month-9 legal opinion; not legal advice.
