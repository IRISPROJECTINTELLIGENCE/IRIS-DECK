IRIS reads the funded project, not the builder’s balance sheet. It can’t say whether a builder will fail; it shows when the builder’s trouble reaches the project, often first as trades paid late.

A2

Builder failure. What the lender recovers is roughly the completed value, less the cost to finish and the cost of delay. Delay is the part a lender can most influence: interest and holding costs run on, presale buyers can rescind at their sunset dates, trades disperse and open work deteriorates. Speed without the position moves the cost into the replacement builder’s price, so the lever is knowing the position fast. A first position in days from access is the design aim; it depends on the state of the records. After an appointment, access to the builder’s systems needs the administrator’s agreement and live software subscriptions, so the strongest case is IRIS on the project before a failure. Scope: IRIS reads the funded project, not the builder’s balance sheet, and makes no solvency call on the builder as a company. A builder short of cash tends to show it on the project first: trades paid late or short, packages left unlet, claims running ahead of work. Queensland’s project trust accounts protect trades’ money on private projects of $10M and over, but they don’t make the builder solvent or give the lender a view, and the Queensland Productivity Commission found they haven’t effectively reduced non-payment (reported).
