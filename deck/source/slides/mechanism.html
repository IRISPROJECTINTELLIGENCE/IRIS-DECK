Left column: what a lender's QS progress drawdown report covers. A typical report has a development cost table (budget, cost to date, cost to complete), work in place valued against the builder's claim, programme and extensions of time, variations as submitted, approvals and permits, insurances, the builder's statutory declaration that subcontractors are paid, risk items and a cash flow forecast. Reports commonly state that the information supplied was not independently verified, and they make no allowance for rectifying defective work. Row 2: the AIQS guidance note for these reports (Construction Financing Reports, 2nd edition, 2019) has the QS assess cost to complete on the footing that the original contractor completes the works, and say whether funding is sufficient to complete. The builder's own cost to finish is outside the report. Row 3: with each claim the builder declares that its subcontractors have been paid what is owed, and the same guidance has the QS obtain that declaration before certifying a drawdown. In Queensland it is a supporting statement under section 75 of the BIF Act (from October 2020), and an amount is owed once its due date has passed (QBCC). ASIC's surveillance of 40 contractors on eight large projects found two that may have signed false declarations, and it said false declarations may signal cash flow problems (16-325MR, September 2016). The payment records show each trade's claims, approvals and payments, against what the builder has itself claimed and been paid for the same work. Right column: the position organised by fundamental cause, so each item grows from a mechanism. 1. Entitlement at the time bar: delay notices, variations, latent conditions and provisional-sum adjustments each carry a notice period; what is approaching or past its bar decides whether the cost is recoverable or the builder's own, with liquidated damages on top. 2. Forecast movement not yet notified: the records show delays, changes, latent conditions and allowance movements coming before any notice is lodged, and each carries consequential cost, most visibly preliminaries that extend with the delay. 3. Contingency and liquid allowances: a large part of forecast final cost sits as builder contingency inside trade budgets; how much of the forecast is still soft is a risk measure in itself. 4. Market exposure in the trades: rates locked at tender are open market risk once labour and material prices move; trades let against allowances and scope still to let are the price risk crystallising or still open. 5. Completion risk: labour on site against the programme, and open defects and rework against practical completion, show whether PC will be reached and when. 6. Cash strain in the trades: a builder paid for work it has not paid its trades for is funding itself from the project; late and short payment of trades is an early sign of a builder short of cash. The QS report sees each of these as submitted, if at all; IRIS reads the records they come from and values and dates each one. The systems: Procore and Aconex are project management platforms and overlap; a project may run one or both. Jobpac is the builder’s ERP: costing, budgeting, forecasting, payroll and contract administration. PayApps handles subcontractors’ payment claims, approval and payment. ProcurePro runs tendering and trade letting, and holds the subcontracts. Email and Office carry the correspondence across all parties, and the spreadsheets where much of the monthly reconciliation is done. The builder’s own team assembles its internal cost report and the PCG report to the client each month. The lender’s QS does substantiate each progress claim: it values work in place from a site inspection and adjusts the claim where it runs ahead of the work. IRIS sits beside that.
