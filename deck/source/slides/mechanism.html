Since 2021, fixed prices, thinner builders and in-house delivery have moved more of the risk inside the project. The lender’s view is still a certificate on the head contract. Six causes and the market shifts: appendix A2, A3.

Left column: what a lender's QS progress drawdown report covers. A typical report has a development cost table (budget, cost to date, cost to complete), work in place valued against the builder's claim, programme and extensions of time, cost to complete on the contract sum, variations as submitted, approvals and permits, insurances, the builder's statutory declaration that subcontractors are paid, risk items and a cash flow forecast. Reports commonly state that the information supplied was not independently verified, and they make no allowance for rectifying defective work. Right column: the position organised by fundamental cause, so each item grows from a mechanism rather than an example. 1. Entitlement at the time bar: delay notices, variations, latent conditions and provisional-sum adjustments each carry a notice period; what is approaching or past its bar decides whether the cost is recoverable or the builder's own, with liquidated damages on top. 2. Forecast movement not yet notified: the records show delays, changes, latent conditions and allowance movements coming before any notice is lodged, and each carries consequential cost, most visibly preliminaries that extend with the delay. 3. Contingency and liquid allowances: a large part of forecast final cost sits as builder contingency inside trade budgets, and latent-condition and provisional-sum allowances are liquid by nature; how much of the forecast is still soft is a risk measure in itself. 4. Market exposure in the trades: rates locked at tender are open market risk once labour and material prices move; trades let against allowances and scope still to let are the price risk already crystallising or still open; the labour market moving, for example to government work, and a subcontractor that leaves for better pay leaves the builder short of labour and the programme in delay. 5. Completion risk: labour numbers on site against the programme, and open defects, rework and non-conformances against practical completion, show whether PC will be reached and when, so the lender can adjust its facility before the date moves. 6. Cash strain in the trades: what subcontractors have claimed and are owed, against what the builder has itself claimed upstream and been paid for the same work. A builder paid for work it has not paid its trades for is funding itself from the project; the statutory declaration says the trades are paid, the payment records show whether they are. This is usually the first sign of a builder in trouble, and it is the one the lender sees last. The QS report sees each of these as submitted, if at all; IRIS reads the records they come from and values and dates each one. The systems: Procore and Aconex are project management platforms and overlap heavily; a project may run one or both. Procore’s core tools cover drawings and documents, RFIs and submittals, site diaries and photos, inspections and defects, meetings and programme, and, in its financial tools, budgets, subcontract commitments and variations. Aconex, owned by Oracle, grew out of document control shared by the client, consultants and builder: a controlled document register with version control, formal mail kept as a permanent record, review and approval workflows and an audit trail that can’t be altered, with field, cost and model tools added since. Jobpac is the builder’s ERP: project costing, budgeting, forecasting, payroll and contract administration. PayApps handles subcontractors’ payment claims, certification and payment. ProcurePro runs tendering and trade letting, and holds the subcontracts. Email and Office carry the day-to-day correspondence across all parties, and the spreadsheets where much of the monthly reconciliation is done. Monthly reporting covers the builder’s internal cost reporting and the PCG report to the client; the builder’s own team assembles both. The lender’s QS does substantiate each progress claim, and that matters: it values work in place from a site inspection and checks the claim against the contract sum. But its inputs are the claim, the builder’s variation and EOT registers as submitted, the statutory declaration that trades are paid, and the builder’s programme; it reports that this information was not independently verified, makes no allowance for defects, and does not see the cost ledger, the unsubmitted changes or the notices beneath the claim. On the three jobs we read, each figure the QS certified equalled what the builder claimed.
